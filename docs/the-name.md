# The name

The dedication makes three historical claims: that the letters S.D.G. come from Bach's manuscripts, that the words were a common formula among churchmen of his time, and that Tolstoy renounced copyright on his later writings. This page carries the references for those claims, and is honest about which parts are firm and which are not.

## What Bach did

Bach opened manuscripts with **J.J.** (*Jesu Juva*, "Jesus help") or **I.N.J.** (*In Nomine Jesu*, "in the name of Jesus"), and closed them with **S.D.G.** — a prayer at the start of the work and an ascription at the end. The practice is consistently attested for his church compositions.

How far it extended into his secular output is less clear. Careful sources say some but not all; the frequently repeated claim that he inscribed it on the *Coffee Cantata* is not something this project has been able to verify from an authoritative source, and it should be treated as unconfirmed.

## It was a convention

This is the part most retellings omit. S.D.G. was not Bach's invention or his private signature. It was standard practice among Lutheran composers of the period.

Handel closed the *Messiah* manuscript with it. Dieterich Buxtehude used it. So did Christoph Graupner. Haydn ran the same structure with different words, opening with *In nomine Domini* and closing with *Laus Deo*, and sometimes *Soli Deo gloria*. Outside music, Cistercian and Trappist monks used the phrase as a written greeting in correspondence. It survives today as an institutional motto — the American Guild of Organists and a number of schools and colleges carry it.

The phrase itself is one of the five *solae* of the Reformation, though the tidy fivefold list is a twentieth-century systematisation popularised around the 1996 Cambridge Declaration; the individual phrases are older than the set.

## Why the project uses it anyway

Because a formula used carelessly by many people is not thereby spoiled, and because the words say something the institutions that used them did not practise.

*Soli Deo gloria* was polemical in origin. Its target was institutional glory-claiming: not to the Church, not to the saints, not to the hierarchy. Read strictly, it leaves nothing over for any institution, any office, or any author. That reading is available in the words themselves and does not depend on what Bach or anyone else meant by them.

This is also Tolstoy's own method. He did not abandon "God," "gospel," or "the kingdom of God" because the church had them in its mouth; he argued they had been taken and took them back. *The Kingdom of God Is Within You* is a gospel phrase reclaimed against the institution claiming to own it. Naming a copyright renunciation *Soli Deo Gloria* is the same move.

## Tolstoy

Tolstoy was declared fallen away from the Orthodox Church by edict of the Holy Synod in February 1901. The stated grounds were that he had produced his own versions of the Gospels with the miracles removed, keeping the moral teaching, and had written *Critique of Dogmatic Theology* against the church's practices. He was not condemned for unbelief but for holding that the church had replaced Jesus's instructions with something else.

On copyright: 1881 is the dividing line. Rights in works published before it went to his wife Sofya Andreyevna; for the later work he resolved to give the rights away, and a will signed in 1909 placed control with Vladimir Chertkov and his daughter Alexandra.

Two honest caveats. The renunciation was partial and bitterly contested within his family for decades, so "he renounced copyright on his later writings" is defensible but tidier than the events. And this project has not verified the specific mechanism by which he made the renunciation public. Anyone wanting to state it more precisely should work from the diaries, letters, and the will itself — published, largely public domain, and preferable to any biographer's account.
